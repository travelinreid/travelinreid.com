What this map can—and can’t—tell you

The map includes documented examples across all eight groups and several offices. For some combinations, the research has established individual cases but has not established a reliable complete historical roster; other states remain marked “not yet verified,” not “never.” Select a state for its status and, where available, a source. People named are examples of representation and are not necessarily the first. Alaska’s Senate race was still being counted at the research cutoff. Mississippi is also unverified because sources differ on Hiram Revels’s Native ancestry: NCpedia describes Native ancestry, while the National Park Service describes it as rumored. Where research is incomplete or a race remains unresolved, the map marks affected states “not yet verified”: a blank is not evidence that representation has never happened. Categories such as veterans and people with disabilities need additional research and clear, consistent definitions before they can be mapped responsibly.

“Openly LGBTQ+” means publicly known as LGBTQ+ during service, including involuntary disclosure; it does not include identity first disclosed after service. The LGBTQ+ Victory Institute identifies the first three openly LGBTQ+ senators, including an appointee, and the 2026 election record identifies Chris Pappas as a senator-elect. A historical review of governors includes Jim McGreevey, who came out while in office. “No documented representation” means our named historical rosters and election sources did not identify a qualifying officeholder; it is not proof of anyone’s private identity. For Native representation we compared an Indigenous political organization’s roster, scholarly research and 2026 election tracking. Washington and Mississippi remain unverified because sources disagree on the ancestry of two former representatives. For Muslim representation, the map counts publicly documented identification during service; religious identity may be private or change over time. Its House sources include testimony by four Muslim members and CAIR’s election tracking. The Jewish category includes documented religious or community identity; Jewish ancestry and religious practice are not interchangeable. For congressional history we compared the Jewish Virtual Library’s Senate and House rosters with 2026 results. Some records require further investigation where a person’s ancestry, religious affiliation, or community identity do not clearly align; those states remain unverified. Native ancestry and tribal citizenship are also distinct. “Had representation” includes people who took office by appointment or succession, not only those elected. Winners in 2026 are marked separately until they take office in 2027; results may await certification. State boundaries are shown with Alaska and Hawaii inset. The District of Columbia and territories are outside this state-by-state comparison.

The Asian American congressional maps use the Senate historian’s roster and the House historian’s state-by-state roster, with Congressional Asian Pacific American Caucus election reporting and later race calls. These rosters include Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders alongside Asian Americans; the map’s positive examples were checked for Asian ancestry. Sources include the Senate roster of American Indian senators, CAIR’s 2026 election tracking, the House roster of Black members, House roster of Hispanic members, 2026 milestones reported by Roll Call and Enlace Latino NC’s reporting on North Carolina, Senate roster of Black senators, Senate roster of Hispanic senators, and Pew Research Center’s state-by-state compilation of Rutgers CAWP data; CAWP 2026 results; U.S. House historical roster; Washington Post historical overview of Black governors; NPR’s 2026 governor results. Research cutoff: November 12, 2026.
