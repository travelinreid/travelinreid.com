The methodology

“First” needs
a footnote.

Who counts, which office, where, and when? How we qualify the question can change the answer.

From “never” to “when”

In 2018, my team at USA TODAY’s Storytelling Studio used a quiz to draw attention to the lack of diversity in elected representation. Elections since then have changed many of the answers (and the company shut down the servers on which all our work lived). The significance of the 2026 mid-term elections prompted me to revisit the project and expand it into a source-linked historical reference.

What this collection covers

The collection focuses on the U.S. Congress, state governors, and selected national, state legislative, local, territorial, and tribal milestones. It includes race and ethnicity, gender, publicly documented LGBTQ identities, religion, and selected disability-related milestones.

It is a curated, expanding reference, not an exhaustive inventory. I have not included every first for every state office; doing so would greatly expand the timeline and the research required. I have included a number of state-level firsts from the 2026 midterm election to capture the significance of those results. Tribal nations are sovereign political communities; their offices are identified separately from state offices. Territorial delegates and resident commissioners are distinguished from voting members of Congress.

An election is not an inauguration

The timeline places each record at its specified event: election, appointment, succession, or beginning of service. Where available, it also provides the election year and service year. A winner of a November 2026 race may not take office until 2027. This becomes important in a few “tie-breaker” cases.

The research cutoff is November 12, 2026. A reported or projected victory is not labeled certified unless certification was verified. Ongoing counts and later corrections may change the collection.

What counts as evidence

Sources include official election returns, congressional and state histories, officeholder biographies, and original research by organizations such as Rutgers’ Center for American Women and Politics. Advocacy organizations’ own milestone tracking is identified as such.

Each record links to the specific evidence and describes what it supports. Historical identity terminology can differ between sources. “Openly” is retained when a claim concerns public identity at the time of election or service. Identity is not inferred from someone’s name, appearance, spouse, etc.

A first-by-election and a first-by-service belong to different people in some cases. Newly documented history can also revise a long-repeated “first.”

The original workbook

The accompanying workbook preserves the 2018 source material and adds a row-by-row audit, updated answers, retirement decisions for dated campaign questions, and an expanded milestone collection. The original supporting rosters are historical snapshots, not current counts.

Some original draft questions contained mistakes; others have become obsolete. Unresolved claims remain visible in the audit and are kept out of the playable question set.

AI disclosure

The Election Never-Land collection of pages was researched and built using OpenAI’s Codex/ChatGPT. I require AI tools to furnish auditable trails and logs for data projects like this; the sources of all data in this project are noted, and the downloadable records include source information. I manually reviewed the earlier collection; newly added research is awaiting my review.
